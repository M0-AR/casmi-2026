🌱 Beginner Guide — from zero to pro

No chemistry or machine-learning background required. Work through these in order — by the end you'll understand this problem better than most people who'd be asked about it in a technical interview.

1. What is a molecule, and why does identifying one matter?

Every chemical substance — water, caffeine, aspirin, a hormone in your blood — is a specific arrangement of atoms. Knowing a molecule's structure (which atoms, connected how) is what lets chemists understand what it does, whether it's safe, and how to make more of it. Discovering a new medicine very often starts with finding an interesting molecule in nature and figuring out exactly what it is.

2. How do you "weigh" a molecule you've never seen before?

A mass spectrometer takes a tiny sample, breaks its molecules into charged fragments, and measures the exact weight and abundance of each fragment. Isolating one molecule and then shattering that molecule to weigh its pieces is called tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), and the result — a list of (fragment weight, abundance) pairs — is called a spectrum. It acts like a fingerprint, but there's no complete, perfect lookup table of every possible molecule's fingerprint.

3. Why is going from "spectrum" to "structure" hard?

Three cases, in increasing order of difficulty:

  1. Already in a reference library — someone has measured this exact compound before. Easy case: find the matching fingerprint.
  2. Structure is known, but no one has measured its spectrum. You infer it by finding structurally similar "cousin" molecules whose spectra are known.
  3. Never been cataloged at all. The genuinely open research problem — unsolved even by the best teams in this competition.

Two completely different molecules can also share the exact same atoms, just connected differently (called isomers), and often produce very similar spectra. Telling those apart turns out to be the single hardest part of this whole problem — see Research & Findings.

4. How do you write a molecule's structure down as text?

SMILES is a compact text encoding of a molecule's atoms and bonds that software can parse (aspirin is CC(=O)OC1=CC=CC=C1C(=O)O). InChIKey is a fixed-length hash computed from a structure, so two different SMILES spellings of the same molecule produce the same key — essential, because the same molecule can be drawn a couple of equally valid ways (called tautomers). This project found that the training data's convenience identity column skips that standardization step — and measured exactly how much score that silently costs if you trust it.

5. So what does this pipeline actually do?
  1. Look it up — compare the unknown spectrum against ~2.5 million spectra with known structures.
  2. Look for cousins — if nothing matches closely, search for similar-but-not-identical spectra and weigh candidates by structural "family resemblance" (Tanimoto similarity between molecular fingerprints).
  3. Rank, best guess first — up to 25 ranked guesses per molecule, scored by how early the right answer appears.

You now know enough to read the rest of this page, and most of RESEARCH.md, without looking anything else up.

🧩 How it works

The actual retrieval pipeline, step by step.

1
Precursor-mass + adduct filter
Narrows ~2.5M training spectra down to plausible candidates within a ±8.5ppm mass window.
↓
2
Spectral similarity (cosine, peak-matched)
Scores every surviving candidate against the query spectrum's actual fragment pattern.
↓
3
Analog propagation (if no strong match)
Falls back to fingerprint-similarity-weighted evidence from spectrally related "cousins."
↓
4
Canonical dedup & ranking
Candidates are deduplicated by scorer-exact InChIKey14 and output best-guess-first, top 25.

Scorer-exact canonicalization

Recomputes every structure's canonical identity the same way the real competition scorer does.

🧪

Analog propagation

Fingerprint-weighted evidence from spectral "cousins" when no exact match exists, independently validated.
